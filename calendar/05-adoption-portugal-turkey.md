# 05 — Portugal and Turkey: the two ends of adoption

*Portugal, one of the earliest adopters (1582), and Turkey/the Ottoman Empire,
one of the latest (1917/1926). Together they bracket the full span of Gregorian
adoption and show the same reform arriving first and last.*

## Portugal — the early end (1582)

Portugal was among the **very first** states to adopt the Gregorian reform, in
the original cohort that switched the moment *Inter gravissimas* took effect
(document 02).

- **Concepts.** Portugal was a Catholic kingdom using the **Julian** calendar and
  the Western (Dionysian) **Anno Domini** year count. It had no distinct
  calendrical apparatus of its own to reconcile — its calendar was the common
  Western Julian one — so it could adopt the papal reform immediately.
- **The switch.** Along with Spain, the Papal States, and Poland-Lithuania,
  Portugal made the change in October 1582: **Thursday 4 October 1582 was
  followed by Friday 15 October 1582** — the original **10-day** deletion
  (document 02). No accumulated-drift surprises: Portugal took the baseline skip.
- **Why it matters here.** Portugal marks the **earliest** point in the ordered
  exception list after the reform itself (README item 3) and the **smallest**
  day-skip (10 days), establishing the low end of the adoption span.

## Turkey / the Ottoman Empire — the late end (1917 / 1926)

The Ottoman Empire and its successor, the Republic of Turkey, sit at the **late**
end, and their concepts are the richest in this document because the Ottoman
state ran **several calendars at once**.

- **Islamic (Hijri) lunar calendar.** A purely **lunar** calendar of 12 months
  (~354 days) counted from the Hijra (622 CE). It makes **no** attempt to track
  the solar year — so its months (and festivals like Ramadan) drift through all
  the seasons over a ~33-year cycle. It governed religious life.
- **The Rumi calendar.** A **fiscal/administrative** calendar the Ottomans built
  on the **Julian** solar reckoning (to keep tax years aligned with the
  agricultural/solar year) while numbering years from the **Hijra** epoch. It was
  thus a hybrid: Julian months, Hijri-based year count — a bespoke reconciliation
  of solar administration with an Islamic era.
- **The switches, in stages:**
  1. **1917 (fiscal).** The Ottoman government moved the Rumi fiscal calendar's
     day-reckoning onto the **Gregorian** rule, removing the Julian/Gregorian
     offset for administrative dates.
  2. **1926 (full civil), Republic of Turkey.** Under Atatürk's reforms the
     Republic adopted the **Gregorian** calendar and **CE year numbering** for
     all civil purposes (effective 1 January 1926), completing the convergence.
     The Hijri calendar remained for religious observance.

- **Why it matters here.** Turkey marks the **latest** major completion of
  Gregorian adoption in this collection (README item 8) and shows the fullest
  pre-existing complexity being resolved into the common civil calendar.

## The bracket, and what carries forward

- **Span.** Portugal (1582, 10-day skip) and Turkey (1926, full civil adoption)
  bracket the ~3½-century span over which the single Gregorian reform propagated
  to the whole world.
- **The recurring settlement.** Turkey, like China (03) and Russia (04), ended
  with a **Gregorian civil calendar** over a **retained religious/cultural
  calendar** (Hijri) — the same two-layer settlement document 99 generalizes.
- **Lunar-only reckoning** (Hijri) enters the collection here: a calendar that
  deliberately does *not* reconcile with the Sun, the complementary case to the
  Greek/Chinese lunisolar and the Julian/Gregorian solar systems — all of which
  the Unified Calendar must be able to name and overlay.
