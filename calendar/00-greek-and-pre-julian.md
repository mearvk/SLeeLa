# 00 — Greek and pre-Julian calendars

*The reckoning the Greeks agreed on, and the Roman calendar that preceded Julius
Caesar's reform. This is the ground from which the Julian baseline (document 01)
was cut.*

## The Greek lunisolar principle

The Greek city-states did not keep a single calendar; each polis ran its own.
But they shared a common *principle*: the calendar was **lunisolar** — months
followed the Moon, and the year was kept in step with the Sun (and thus the
agricultural and festival seasons) by periodically inserting an extra month.

Core concepts the Greeks fixed and handed forward:

- **Lunar month.** A month is one cycle of the Moon, about 29.5 days, so months
  alternated between 29 ("hollow") and 30 ("full") days.
- **The intercalary month.** Twelve lunar months (~354 days) fall ~11 days short
  of the solar year, so an entire thirteenth month was added in some years to
  prevent the seasons from drifting through the calendar.
- **The Metonic cycle (Meton of Athens, 432 BCE).** The key Greek contribution:
  235 lunar months almost exactly equal 19 solar years. Distributing 7
  intercalary months across each 19-year cycle keeps lunar months and the solar
  year aligned to within a couple of hours per cycle. This 19-year rule is still
  the arithmetic skeleton behind computations like the date of Easter.
- **Local epochs and archon years.** Years were named or numbered locally (e.g.
  by the eponymous archon at Athens), and Greek historians later reckoned in
  **Olympiads** — four-year intervals counted from 776 BCE — giving a shared
  chronological frame across the otherwise independent city calendars.

The Greek achievement, for our purposes, is the **lunisolar reconciliation**:
the explicit, arithmetical idea that a civil calendar must be periodically
corrected against the Sun. Every later reform in this collection is a different
answer to that same reconciliation problem.

## The Roman pre-Julian calendar (the problem to be fixed)

Rome inherited a lunisolar scheme but ran it badly:

- A year of 12 months totalling 355 days, with an intercalary month
  (*Mensis Intercalaris* / *Mercedonius*) inserted irregularly to catch up to
  the solar year.
- Intercalation was **decided by the pontifices** (the priestly college) and
  became politically manipulated — lengthened to keep allies in office, omitted
  for convenience — so the calendar drifted badly out of phase with the seasons.
- By the time of Caesar the civil calendar was roughly **three months ahead** of
  the solar seasons. The year 46 BCE was stretched to 445 days (the "year of
  confusion") to reset the alignment before the new system began.

This is the condition document 01 resolves: the Julian calendar replaced manual,
abusable lunisolar intercalation with a fixed, purely solar leap rule.

## What carries forward

- **Lunisolar reconciliation** (Greek): the governing idea that the civil year
  must be corrected against the Sun — later re-expressed as leap days.
- **The Metonic 19-year cycle** (Greek): survives in ecclesiastical computus.
- **The failure mode of discretionary intercalation** (Roman): the motivation
  for a mechanical, non-political leap rule — delivered by Julius Caesar.
