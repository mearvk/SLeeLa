## 1. Overview

The ICC is a permanent international court seated in The Hague, Netherlands. It was established by the Rome Statute, adopted on 17 July 1998 and in force from 1 July 2002. It prosecutes individuals—not states—for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and the crime of aggression, where statutory jurisdiction and admissibility requirements are met. It complements national criminal jurisdictions rather than replacing them.

The Court has no independent police force. Arrests, transfers, evidence gathering, asset measures, and sentence enforcement depend substantially on state cooperation. The ICC is separate from the International Court of Justice (ICJ), which principally resolves legal disputes between states and gives advisory opinions.

Principal sources: [ICC official website](https://www.icc-cpi.int/), [Core Legal Texts](https://www.icc-cpi.int/publications/core-legal-texts), [Cases](https://www.icc-cpi.int/cases), and [Situations under investigation](https://www.icc-cpi.int/situations-under-investigations).

## 2. What the ICC Is and Is Not

| Question | Explanation |
|---|---|
| What is it? | A permanent international criminal court created by treaty. |
| Who may be prosecuted? | Natural persons, subject to jurisdiction and admissibility rules. |
| What crimes does it address? | Genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and aggression. |
| Where is it seated? | The Hague, Netherlands; the Statute permits sittings elsewhere when appropriate. |
| Is it a UN court? | No. It is independent, with a formal relationship agreement with the UN. |
| Is it the ICJ? | No. Their mandates, procedures, and parties differ. |
| Does it replace domestic courts? | No. Complementarity gives genuine national proceedings a central role. |
| Does it have its own police force? | No. It relies on cooperation for arrests and many enforcement functions. |
| Does an investigation mean guilt? | No. Allegations, investigations, warrants, confirmed charges, judgments, and convictions are different statuses. |
| Does a warrant prove guilt? | No. A warrant is a judicial measure based on a legal threshold, not a conviction. |

## 3. Jurisdiction and Admissibility

A serious allegation alone does not automatically give the ICC jurisdiction. Each matter must be assessed under the Rome Statute and relevant judicial decisions.

| Issue | What to verify |
|---|---|
| Subject matter | Whether conduct falls within a crime defined by the Statute and relevant amendments. |
| Time | Alleged conduct dates, entry into force of the Statute or applicable amendments, and any situation-specific limits. The Statute generally entered into force on 1 July 2002. |
| Territory or nationality | Whether conduct occurred on the territory of a State Party or a state accepting jurisdiction, or whether the relevant person is a national of such a state, subject to the Statute. |
| Security Council referral | Whether the UN Security Council referred the situation under Chapter VII of the UN Charter. |
| State acceptance or referral | Whether a valid declaration or referral applies to the situation and period. |
| Aggression | Apply its distinct jurisdictional conditions; do not assume the rules for the other crimes apply identically. |
| Admissibility | Consider gravity and whether genuine national proceedings render the case inadmissible under complementarity. |
| Interests of justice | Apply the Statute and relevant prosecutorial framework, not personal opinion. |

### Complementarity and Withdrawal

The ICC is designed to complement genuine national investigations and prosecutions. A case may be inadmissible where a state with jurisdiction is genuinely investigating or prosecuting it, or has genuinely investigated and made a qualifying decision, subject to statutory exceptions. Unwillingness or inability genuinely to carry out proceedings can change the analysis. Complementarity is a legal test, not a general rating of a national justice system.

Do not infer jurisdiction merely because a country is in the news, is or was a State Party, or is geographically close to an affected state. Verify conduct dates, treaty status, declarations, amendments, referrals, and official decisions. Withdrawal from the Statute does not automatically erase all jurisdiction over prior conduct; the effect depends on the Statute and facts.

## 4. The Four Core Crimes

The authoritative definitions and elements are in the Rome Statute and the official *Elements of Crimes*. These summaries are not substitutes for the legal text.

### Genocide
Genocide requires specified acts committed with the specific intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group as such. Listed acts include killing group members, causing serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately inflicting destructive conditions of life, imposing measures intended to prevent births, and forcibly transferring children of the group to another group.

**Distinction:** Mass killing or another grave atrocity is not automatically genocide. The required intent and other legal elements must be established.

### Crimes Against Humanity
These involve specified acts committed as part of a widespread or systematic attack directed against a civilian population, with the required knowledge of the attack. Listed acts include murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation or forcible transfer, imprisonment or severe deprivation of liberty, torture, certain sexual crimes, persecution, enforced disappearance, apartheid, and other inhumane acts, subject to the Statute’s definitions.

**Distinction:** The contextual element matters. A serious offence is not automatically a crime against humanity simply because it is grave.

### War Crimes
War crimes are serious violations of applicable law in an international or non-international armed conflict, as defined by the Statute. Provisions include grave breaches and other serious violations, with detailed rules concerning victims, conduct, protected objects, mental elements, and conflict classification. Examples include murder or torture of protected persons, intentionally directing attacks against civilians, certain attacks on protected buildings, pillage, certain sexual crimes, and conscripting or enlisting children under 15 or using them to participate actively in hostilities.

**Distinction:** The armed conflict and applicable legal classification must be established. Not every unlawful act during wartime meets the elements of an ICC war crime.

### Crime of Aggression
This concerns planning, preparation, initiation, or execution by a person effectively able to control or direct a state’s political or military action of an act of aggression that, by its character, gravity, and scale, constitutes a manifest violation of the UN Charter. The Statute defines acts of aggression by reference to a state’s use of armed force against another state’s sovereignty, territorial integrity, or political independence, or otherwise inconsistently with the Charter.

**Distinction:** Aggression has special jurisdictional and procedural conditions. Verify them independently.

## 5. Applicable Law

The official [Core Legal Texts collection](https://www.icc-cpi.int/publications/core-legal-texts) provides the central instruments and related codes. Check the current official edition and amendments.

| Instrument | Function |
|---|---|
| Rome Statute | Founding treaty: jurisdiction, crimes, individual responsibility, institutions, procedure, cooperation, and penalties. |
| Rules of Procedure and Evidence | Procedures for investigations, hearings, trials, evidence, appeals, cooperation, and enforcement. |
| Elements of Crimes | Helps interpret and apply statutory crime definitions by identifying elements. |
| Regulations of the Court | Internal judicial and administrative procedures within the Statute and Rules framework. |
| Regulations of the Registry | Governs Registry functions and administration. |
| Regulations of the Office of the Prosecutor | Governs aspects of the Prosecutor’s office and work. |
| Professional and ethics codes | Standards for counsel, prosecutors, and judicial officers. |
| Judicial decisions and orders | Case-specific rulings and interpretations; check chamber, date, appeal status, and whether superseded. |
| Applicable international law | Article 21 governs applicable law; not every general proposition is automatically dispositive in an ICC case. |

The Statute is the primary treaty. Supporting instruments operate within their prescribed hierarchy and amendment procedures.

## 6. Institutional Structure

| Institution | Principal role |
|---|---|
| Assembly of States Parties (ASP) | Management oversight and legislative functions under the Statute; elects judges and the Prosecutor and approves the budget. |
| Presidency | External relations, judicial coordination, assignment of judges and matters to divisions, and oversight of Registry administration. |
| Judicial Divisions | Pre-Trial, Trial, and Appeals divisions conduct judicial proceedings. The Statute provides for 18 judges, subject to election and composition rules. |
| Office of the Prosecutor (OTP) | Conducts preliminary examinations, investigations, and prosecutions independently under the Statute. |
| Registry | Non-judicial administration, including language services, security, victim and witness support, outreach, and support for defence and victims’ legal representatives. |
| Trust Fund for Victims (TFV) | Separate institution in the Rome Statute system; assists victims and implements Court-ordered reparations within its mandate. |

Officeholders, staff, budgets, and organizational arrangements can change. Use official current pages rather than old rosters.

## 7. Procedure and Case Stages

Not every situation follows an identical path. This outline is general:

1. **Information and preliminary examination:** the Prosecutor considers jurisdiction, statutory requirements, gravity, complementarity, and other criteria.
2. **Investigation:** where permitted, the OTP gathers and assesses evidence, including incriminating and exonerating material as required by the Statute.
3. **Judicial measures:** the Prosecutor may request a warrant of arrest or summons to appear when legal requirements are met.
4. **Arrest or appearance:** states execute warrants and transfer suspects; the ICC does not generally try accused persons in their absence.
5. **Initial appearance:** identity, rights, and procedural matters are addressed.
6. **Confirmation of charges:** the Pre-Trial Chamber assesses whether evidence meets the statutory threshold for trial.
7. **Trial:** the Prosecution must prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt; the Defence may contest charges and evidence, and victims may participate through legal representatives under applicable rules.
8. **Judgment and sentence:** judges decide the case and, where there is a conviction, determine sentence under the Statute.
9. **Reparations:** the Court may issue orders where legally applicable.
10. **Appeal or revision:** available only under relevant legal conditions and procedures.
11. **Enforcement:** imprisonment is served in a designated state under applicable arrangements; enforcement depends on cooperation.

| Status | Meaning and caution |
|---|---|
| Situation | Geographic or factual context in which alleged crimes are examined; not a finding of individual guilt. |
| Investigation | Evidence gathering; does not establish guilt. |
| Suspect | Person suspected of involvement before charges have been confirmed, depending on context. |
| Warrant of arrest | Judicial order based on the applicable legal standard; not a conviction. |
| Summons to appear | Judicial order requiring appearance; not a finding of guilt. |
| Confirmation of charges | Decision that charges meet the threshold to proceed; not a conviction. |
| Accused | Person facing charges in the trial process, according to procedural stage. |
| Convicted | Person found guilty by a competent chamber; check appeal status and finality. |
| Acquitted | Person found not guilty of the charge(s) at issue. |
| Proceedings terminated | Proceedings ended on a stated legal or factual basis; not automatically equivalent to conviction or acquittal. |
| Case closed | Database status that must be read alongside the underlying decision and later appeal or reopening information. |

## 8. Rights, Victims, Witnesses, and Reparations

### Fair-trial Protections
The Statute provides guarantees including the presumption of innocence, notice of charges, time and facilities to prepare a defence, counsel, interpretation and translation where necessary, rights concerning witnesses subject to the rules, and protection against compelled self-incrimination. The Prosecution bears the burden of proving guilt beyond reasonable doubt.

### Victim Participation and Protection
Victims may present views and concerns through legal representatives when their personal interests are affected and procedures permit. Participation is not the same as being a party to a national civil action and does not remove the Prosecution’s burden of proof. Protective measures and confidentiality may apply.

### Reparations and the TFV
Distinguish a judicial reparations order, a TFV assistance programme, a proposed or pending reparations plan, an award or eligibility decision, and actual delivery of assistance. Do not state that a victim received compensation unless an authoritative source supports it.

### Age and Responsibility
The ICC does not have jurisdiction over a person who was under 18 at the time of the alleged crime. This is distinct from rules concerning recruitment or use of children in armed conflict.

## 9. Cooperation and Enforcement

The ICC relies on states and partners for arrest and surrender, evidence and witness assistance, service of documents, protection, asset measures, sentence enforcement, and other judicial assistance.

| Issue | Responsible research approach |
|---|---|
| Warrant | Check the decision, public/sealed status, and later withdrawal, amendment, or termination orders. |
| Arrest | Do not infer arrest from a warrant; seek official confirmation of arrest, surrender, or appearance. |
| State cooperation | Check official filings and state/ASP statements. Separate a judicial finding of non-cooperation from commentary about motives. |
| Sentence | Check appeal status, designation of a state, and whether transfer occurred. |
| Confidential material | Do not expose sealed or protected information; record only lawfully public information. |
| Sanctions or diplomatic measures | Attribute statements to the issuing authority and date; distinguish government action from judicial findings and jurisdictional change. |

## 10. Current Cases and Situations

The [official cases index](https://www.icc-cpi.int/cases) provides live case records and filters such as warrant/summons, alleged crime, procedural stage, and defendant status. The [situations index](https://www.icc-cpi.int/situations-under-investigations) provides situation-level information.

Official public pages include matters such as Afghanistan, Darfur (Sudan), the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Libya, Mali, the Central African Republic and Central African Republic II, Côte d’Ivoire, Uganda, Kenya, the Philippines, Venezuela I, Ukraine, and others. This is illustrative, not exhaustive, and does not imply that each matter remains at the same stage. Matters can be appealed, concluded, reopened, or otherwise changed.

For each case record the official situation name; case and person name; alleged conduct dates and place; current stage; charges and statutory provisions; warrant/summons dates; arrest or appearance confirmation; chamber and decision details; appeal status; outcome; last verification date; and source URL. Prefer the underlying judgment, order, or filing over a summary entry.

## 11. States Parties and Amendments

The [ASP Rome Statute and other agreements page](https://asp.icc-cpi.int/RomeStatute) provides treaty texts, amendments, and participation information. The official page observed for this research reported **125 States Parties**, with a displayed update date of 10 July 2026. Recheck the figure before publication or legal use.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| State Party | A state for which the Rome Statute is in force, subject to treaty rules and dates. |
| Ratification/accession | Formal treaty processes by which a state consents to be bound. |
| Declaration accepting jurisdiction | May accept ICC jurisdiction for specified conduct or periods, subject to legal requirements. |
| State referral | A State Party may refer a situation to the Prosecutor under the Statute. |
| Security Council referral | A referral under the UN Charter can provide a distinct jurisdictional route. |
| Withdrawal | Governed by the Statute’s withdrawal provision and effective date; does not automatically undo all prior jurisdiction or proceedings. |
| Amendment participation | Participation in a particular amendment may differ from general participation in the Statute. Check each amendment’s text, entry into force, and acceptance status. |

The ASP page lists amendments, including amendments relating to Article 8, aggression, and Article 124. Do not assume every amendment binds every State Party identically.

## 12. Relationship with the UN and Other Courts

The ICC is not a UN organ. The Statute provides for a relationship agreement with the UN. The Security Council can refer situations under Chapter VII, and the Court interacts with UN bodies under specific legal and institutional arrangements.

| ICC | ICJ |
|---|---|
| International criminal jurisdiction over individuals. | Principal UN judicial organ; resolves disputes between states and gives advisory opinions. |
| Created by the Rome Statute. | Established under the UN Charter and ICJ Statute. |
| May try accused individuals and impose criminal sentences within its mandate. | Does not conduct criminal trials of individuals or impose criminal sentences. |
| Depends on state cooperation for arrests and enforcement. | Operates under its own framework for judgments, advisory opinions, and state compliance. |

The ICC is distinct from the ICTY, ICTR, IRMCT, hybrid or internationalized courts, and national courts. Their mandates, jurisdiction, legal instruments, and current status differ; consult each institution’s official records.

## 13. Research and Evidence Handling

### Source Hierarchy
Use sources in this general order, depending on the question:
1. Rome Statute and applicable treaty amendments.
2. Rules of Procedure and Evidence and other core legal texts.
3. Official ICC judgments, decisions, orders, warrants, filings, and case pages.
4. Official ASP, Registry, OTP, and TFV publications.
5. Official UN documents, including Security Council resolutions and reports.
6. National legislation, court records, and official state statements relevant to cooperation or domestic proceedings.
7. Scholarly commentary and reputable legal analysis, identified as secondary sources.
8. News reporting and civil-society publications, attributed and cross-checked.
9. Social-media posts, anonymous claims, and unsourced summaries: leads only, not proof.

An official source may contain a party’s allegation rather than a judicial finding. A Prosecutor’s filing is not automatically a court determination.

### Evidence Metadata and Chain of Custody

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Evidence identifier | Stable, unambiguous record ID. |
| Description | Neutral description that does not assume authenticity or truth. |
| Origin/source | Who supplied or published it and under what circumstances. |
| Collection date/time | Include timezone where known; distinguish collection from publication. |
| Original location | Preserve source URL, document number, page, or archive reference. |
| File integrity | SHA-256 or stronger approved hash, with tool and time recorded. |
| Custody events | Append-only log of access, transfer, conversion, redaction, and storage. |
| Authentication status | Unverified, partially verified, or verified under a stated method. |
| Corroboration | Independent supporting or contradicting sources. |
| Restrictions | Confidentiality, consent, privilege, protective orders, personal data, and legal restrictions. |
| Analyst notes | Separate observations, inferences, and unresolved questions. |

A cryptographic hash can help show that a file has not changed since it was hashed. It does **not** establish that the file is authentic, complete, true, or admissible. A chain-of-custody log does not itself guarantee admissibility.

Cite documents with title, issuing body, date, case/situation, document number where available, pinpoint paragraph or page, URL, and access date. Preserve the version consulted. If a document is amended, superseded, corrected, or translated, retain the earlier citation and identify the version supporting each claim.

## 14. Responsible Reporting

- Presume innocence unless and until guilt is established by a competent court.
- Describe conduct as “alleged” while it remains an allegation.
- Attribute claims: “the Prosecutor alleges,” “the Defence submits,” or “the Chamber found.”
- Do not label a person a war criminal solely because a warrant exists or an investigation is open.
- Distinguish a Prosecutor’s application from a judicial decision.
- Distinguish a warrant from an arrest, confirmation of charges from conviction, and a conviction under appeal from a final judgment.
- Report acquittals, withdrawn charges, terminated proceedings, and exculpatory information as carefully as allegations.
- Avoid exposing identifying details about victims, witnesses, children, or protected persons where doing so may create risk or violate an order.
- Do not treat ethnicity, nationality, religion, political affiliation, or public prominence as evidence of individual criminal responsibility.
- Avoid sensationalism and unsupported claims about motive, guilt, or institutional independence.
- For disputed legal conclusions, identify the source and relevant counter-position and cite the primary record.

Each stored claim should include a claim ID, claim text, claim type, subject, event date, publication date, source authority, source URL, document ID, pinpoint reference, status-as-of date, verification state, reviewer, and review date. Controlled claim types should distinguish allegation, party submission, prosecutorial request, judicial finding, final judgment, secondary analysis, and unknown. Do not collapse them into a generic “fact” label.

Minimum acceptance tests:
- Every legal-status claim has a primary-source citation or explains why one is unavailable.
- Every case status includes a date last checked.
- Allegations are distinguishable from judicial findings.
- Judgment records identify appeal status where known.
- Documents have stable identifiers and, where practical, integrity hashes.
- Link failure is not treated as proof that a record never existed.
- Protected data is not exposed through search, logs, examples, or errors.
- Source updates do not silently overwrite prior status snapshots.
- Public pages identify the software as independent and not an official ICC service.

## 16. Security and Integrity

“Secure” is a system property requiring implemented controls and testing; a heading or signature in Markdown does not make a file secure.

1. **Minimize data:** prefer public records and metadata. Do not ingest confidential evidence or personal data without a documented legal basis and authorization.
2. **Protect sensitive records:** use least-privilege access, encryption at rest and in transit, controlled secrets, backups, and retention limits where sensitive data is handled.
3. **Verify source files:** record hashes for downloaded documents and preserve acquisition timestamps and source URLs.
4. **Use trusted references:** prefer official HTTPS domains and verify document numbers and signatures where available.
5. **Maintain provenance:** separate original files from derived copies, redactions, OCR, translations, and analyst notes.
6. **Keep audit trails:** record who changed a record, when, why, and the prior version. Never commit passwords, access tokens, or private keys.
7. **Review software:** apply code review, dependency checks, access control, secret scanning, and reproducible build/test procedures.
8. **Handle disclosures responsibly:** do not publish sealed materials, personal addresses, witness identifiers, or information likely to endanger people.
9. **Use hashes accurately:** hashes detect changes relative to a recorded digest; they do not prove source authenticity or truth.
10. **Separate status from assurance:** “Complete” means the planned reference-document scope is filled out for this edition. It does not mean all Internet content is captured, all links remain live, or a formal security audit has passed.

No claim is made that this repository, document, or software has undergone an independent security audit or received ICC approval.

## 18. Limitations and Maintenance

This document is complete as a **general reference edition** based on the official sources listed above, not an exhaustive database of every ICC document, case, filing, statement, academic article, media report, or Internet page. No static file can truthfully guarantee that it contains “all information on the Internet.”

Before relying on it for a specific case, legal filing, public statement, or software decision:
1. Open the current official case/situation page.
2. Retrieve the latest relevant judicial decision and any appeal decision.
3. Check the applicable Statute, amendments, Rules, and Elements of Crimes.
4. Confirm the date, status, and source of every material factual claim.
5. Record the verification date and update the source snapshot.
6. Review confidentiality, privacy, copyright, and protective-order restrictions.
7. Consult qualified counsel where legal rights or obligations are at stake.

Recommended maintenance:
- Case status: check before each use and on a scheduled review cycle.
- Treaty participation and amendments: check ASP and UN treaty records before citing numbers or legal effect.
- Legal texts: check when amendments or new editions are published.
- Links and schemas: run periodic link, metadata, and test validation.
- Annual review: review this reference at least once per year and record changes in Git history.
