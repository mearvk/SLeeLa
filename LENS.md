> A **Lens**, in Sleela, is a composable *view*: a `get` that focuses on a
> part of a larger whole and a `set` that puts a changed part back without
> losing the rest (SL-META-0001 §2.2; see [`GLOSSARY.md`](GLOSSARY.md)). This
> document borrows that idea as a framing device: it focuses a single lens on
> one subject — *how the United States was created* — and reports what the
> documented record shows through four apertures:
>
> 1. **Who built it** — the peoples, labor, and institutions.
> 2. **Who bought it** — the land the republic acquired by purchase and treaty.
> 3. **How it was governed into being** — founding documents and amendments.
> 4. **Early reliefs — 1967** — a focused year of civil-rights relief and reform.
>
> **Method note.** This file follows the discipline of the `politico/` SAKE
> documents: state facts as facts, mark interpretation as interpretation, and
> never turn one into the other. Dated, attributable claims carry a source tag
> like `[S1]`; see [Sources](#sources). Where a statement is a framing or a

## 1. The lens, stated

A nation is not a single object; it is a composition. To ask *how America was
created* is to ask how many separate parts — land, people, law, labor, and
time — were focused into one whole. A lens does not invent its subject; it
selects what to bring into focus and leaves the rest legible in the
background. The apertures below are deliberately narrow so that each claim can
be checked against a source.

- **Get.** From the whole *United States*, focus one part (a people, a
  purchase, a law) and read what the record says about it.
- **Set.** Return that part to the whole without erasing the others: no single
  aperture is the entire story. *(interpretation)*

The republic dated its own beginning to 1776 in declaration and to 1788 in
ratified government [S10]. Everything after is composition: more land, more
people, and more law focused onto that frame.

## 2. Who built it

"Built" has more than one honest meaning. It includes the framers who wrote
the government; the many peoples already present on the land; the free and
enslaved laborers whose work made the economy; and the successive waves of
immigrants who enlarged it. A lens that names only one of these misreports the
whole. *(interpretation)*

### Indigenous nations

Hundreds of distinct nations and confederacies inhabited and governed the land for millennia before European arrival; their presence, dispossession, and treaties are part of how the territory came under U.S. control [S1].

### The framers and founders

The delegates who drafted the Declaration (1776) and the Constitution (1787) built the legal architecture of the republic [S10].

### Enslaved and free laborers

The labor of enslaved people — legally recognized until the Thirteenth Amendment (1865) — and of free workers built much of the early agrarian and industrial economy [S10].

### Immigrant generations

Successive waves of immigration supplied the labor and population that filled the territory the republic acquired [S1].

### Settlers and westward migrants

Driven in part by the ideology later called Manifest Destiny, settlers moved into acquired territories, converting purchase and treaty into occupied states [S5].

### The Congress and the courts

Statute and judicial decision built the ongoing structure — admitting states, defining rights, and resolving disputes over the acquired land.

**Aperture summary.** *Who built it?* No single group. The honest answer is a
composition: prior nations, founders, laborers (free and enslaved),
immigrants, settlers, and the institutions of law. *(interpretation)*

## 3. Who bought it — the land acquisitions

Part of America was *bought*. Between 1803 and 1970 the United States paid, by
one estimate, no less than about $2.6 billion for territory (roughly $65
billion in later dollars) [S9]. The largest single acquisition was the
Louisiana Purchase of 1803, for about $10 million, which nearly doubled the
land area of the original thirteen states [S7]. The most famous later purchase
was Alaska, in 1867, from Russia, for $7.2 million [S8].

The table below lists the principal *purchases and paid cessions*. Acquisitions
by war, annexation, or treaty without payment are noted separately.

| Year | Acquisition | From | Payment (nominal) | Note | Source |
|------|-------------|------|-------------------|------|--------|
| 1803 | Louisiana Purchase | France | ~$10 million | Nearly doubled the U.S. land area | [S7] |
| 1819 | Florida (Adams–Onís) | Spain | ~$5 million in assumed claims | Cession via treaty | [S1] |
| 1848 | Mexican Cession | Mexico | $15M + $3M assumed claims | Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo | [S4] |
| 1853 | Gadsden Purchase | Mexico | $10 million | ~18.99M acres, southern AZ/NM | [S3] |
| 1867 | Alaska Purchase | Russia | $7.2 million | ~378M acres | [S3][S8] |
| 1917 | U.S. Virgin Islands | Denmark | $25 million | Purchased territory | [S6] |

### On the phrase "who bought it"

The buyer, in every case above, was the **United States government**, acting
by treaty and appropriation — not a private owner. The sellers were other
states (France, Spain, Mexico, Russia, Denmark). This is the precise sense in
which parts of America were "bought": sovereign purchase of territory, ratified
by treaty and paid from the public treasury [S2][S3].

### What purchase did not settle

A treaty transferred sovereignty on paper; it did not by itself settle the
claims of the peoples already living on the land. Those were addressed — often
unjustly — through separate treaties, statutes, and force over the following
century [S1]. A lens that shows only the purchase price omits this. *(interpretation)*

## 4. How it was governed into being

Land and people become a *nation* through law. The documents below are the
load-bearing frame onto which every later part was set.

### 1776 — Declaration of Independence

Declared the separation of the thirteen colonies from Britain and stated the republic's founding principles [S10].

### 1781 — Articles of Confederation

The first national frame of government; too weak to hold the union, it was replaced within a decade [S10].

### 1787 — Constitution drafted

The Constitutional Convention produced the governing charter of the United States [S10].

### 1788 — Constitution ratified

Ratification brought the federal government into operation [S10].

### 1791 — Bill of Rights

The first ten amendments guaranteed core individual rights [S10].

### 1865 — Thirteenth Amendment

Abolished slavery, ending the legal status that had underwritten much early labor [S10].

### 1868 — Fourteenth Amendment

Guaranteed birthright citizenship and equal protection of the laws [S10].

### 1967 — Twenty-fifth Amendment

Ratified in 1967, it clarified presidential succession and disability, and the filling of a vice-presidential vacancy [S9-25].

## 6. Early reliefs — 1967

You asked the lens to focus on **1967**. The honest focus is this: 1967 was
not a year in which America was founded or purchased — those apertures close
in the 1700s and 1800s. It was a year of *relief and reform* — legal and
constitutional steps that eased long-standing injustices — set against a
summer of unrest that made the need for relief plain. The record below is
factual and dated; the framing of these as "reliefs" is *(interpretation)*.

### 6.1 Constitutional relief

- **Twenty-fifth Amendment (ratified February 10, 1967).** Clarified that the
  vice president becomes president on the president's death, resignation, or
  removal, and set procedures for filling a vice-presidential vacancy and for
  presidential disability [S9-25]. It relieved a genuine gap in the
  succession rules.

### 6.2 Judicial relief

- **Loving v. Virginia (decided June 12, 1967).** The Supreme Court struck
  down state bans on interracial marriage as unconstitutional under the Equal
  Protection and Due Process Clauses [S3-L][S8-L]. It relieved couples of laws
  that had criminalized their marriages.

### 6.3 The summer that showed the need

- **The "long, hot summer" of 1967.** Over the first nine months of 1967,
  scores of civil disturbances — reported as roughly 150–158 — erupted in
  American cities, the most destructive in Detroit, Michigan, and Newark,
  New Jersey [S2][S7-R].
- **Newark (July 12–17, 1967).** At least 26 people died and hundreds were
  seriously injured, with heavy property damage [S5].
- **The Kerner Commission.** President Johnson appointed a national commission
  to examine the causes of the unrest; its report became a landmark analysis
  of racial inequality [S7-R]. *(context)*

### 6.4 Cold-War relief abroad

- **Outer Space Treaty (signed January 27, 1967).** The United States, the
  Soviet Union, and the United Kingdom signed the treaty barring nuclear
  weapons in orbit and claims of national sovereignty over celestial bodies
  [S6-T]. It relieved a dangerous ambiguity in the early space race.

### 6.5 Why "reliefs" is the right word here — and its limit

Each item above *removed* a specific burden: a gap in succession, a ban on
marriage, an unregulated arms race in orbit. That is the sense of "relief."
The limit of the lens: relief is not the same as resolution. The 1967 summer
showed that legal relief alone did not end the underlying inequality. *(interpretation)*

## 7. Reading the lens back (get/set)

Applying the get/set discipline of a Sleela Lens to the whole:

- **get("who built it")** → prior nations + founders + laborers (free and
  enslaved) + immigrants + settlers + institutions. [S1][S10]
- **get("who bought it")** → the U.S. government, by treaty and appropriation,
  from France, Spain, Mexico, Russia, and Denmark. [S2][S3][S6][S7][S8]
- **get("1967")** → constitutional, judicial, and diplomatic *relief*, against
  a summer of unrest. [S2][S3-L][S6-T][S9-25]
- **set(...)** → return each part to the whole without letting one aperture
  stand for the entire creation. *(interpretation)*

A well-behaved lens obeys two laws (SL-META-0001 §5, SEM-04): reading after
writing returns what you wrote, and writing back what you read changes
nothing. Applied here: the facts you focus on must survive being put back into
the full account unchanged. If a framing only holds when the rest of the
record is hidden, it fails the law. *(interpretation)*

## Appendix E — 1967 in fuller focus

Because the lens is asked to dwell on **1967**, here are additional dated
anchors from that year, each a checkable fact with its framing kept separate.

### 1967 · January 27

The United States, the Soviet Union, and the United Kingdom sign the Outer Space Treaty. [S6-T]

*Lens note:* A Cold-War *relief*: it barred weapons of mass destruction in orbit.

### 1967 · February 10

The Twenty-fifth Amendment is ratified. [S9-25]

*Lens note:* A constitutional *relief*: it fixed the succession and disability gap.

### 1967 · Spring–summer

Civil disturbances build across American cities, reported as roughly 150–158 over the first nine months. [S2-R][S7-R]

*Lens note:* The unrest that made the need for relief plain. *(context)*

### 1967 · June 12

Loving v. Virginia is decided. [S3-L][S8-L]

*Lens note:* A judicial *relief*: interracial-marriage bans held unconstitutional.

### 1967 · July 12–17

Newark experiences several days of unrest; at least 26 die. [S5]

*Lens note:* The human cost behind the year's reform pressure. *(context)*

### 1967 · July (Detroit)

Detroit sees the summer's most destructive unrest. [S2-R][S7-R]

*Lens note:* Cited ever after as a turning point in the national conversation.

### 1967 · Late July

President Johnson appoints the Kerner Commission to study the causes of the disorders. [S7-R]

*Lens note:* Its report named structural inequality as a root cause. *(context)*

### 1967 · Throughout 1967

Lyndon B. Johnson serves as president across all of the above. [S9-25]

*Lens note:* The executive frame within which the year's reliefs occurred.

### 1967 · Context · succession

Before the Twenty-fifth Amendment, the rules for a disabled president and for filling a vice-presidential vacancy were unsettled. [S9-25]

*Lens note:* This is the specific gap the 1967 ratification relieved.

### 1967 · Context · marriage law

Before June 12, several states still criminalized interracial marriage. [S3-L][S8-L]

*Lens note:* This is the specific burden Loving v. Virginia relieved.

### 1967 · Context · orbit

Before the treaty, no binding rule barred nuclear weapons in Earth orbit. [S6-T]

*Lens note:* This is the specific danger the Outer Space Treaty relieved.

## Closing

One lens, four apertures. America was **built** by many hands, **bought** in
part by sovereign purchase of territory, **governed into being** by written
charters and twenty-seven amendments, and — in the focused year of **1967** —
**relieved** in specific, documented ways, even as a hard summer showed how
much relief still remained to be won.

The facts above are the record. The framing is the lens. This document keeps
the two apart on purpose, because a view earns trust only when each focused
fact survives being set back into the whole unchanged.
