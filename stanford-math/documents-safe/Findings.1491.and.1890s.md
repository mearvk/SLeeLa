## 1. Books / literature from 1890s southern France

The dominant literary movement of southern France in this period was the
**Félibrige** — the revival of the Occitan (Provençal) language, founded 1854 at
Château de Font-Ségugne by seven poets (Mistral, Roumanille, Aubanel, Mathieu,
Brunet, Tavan, Giéra). Its central figure, **Frédéric Mistral** (1830–1914,
Maillane, Provence), won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1904.

Mistral's works, with publication years (Musée Mistral, Maillane):

| Year | Title (Provençal) | French / English |
|------|-------------------|------------------|
| 1859 | *Mirèio* | *Mireille* — narrative poem |
| 1867 | *Calendau* | *Calendal* |
| 1875 | *Lis Isclo d'Or* | *Les Îles d'Or* — poems |
| 1879 | *Lou Tresor dóu Felibrige* | the great Provençal–French dictionary (2 vols, 1879–1886; Internet Archive copies dated 1879) |
| 1884 | *Nerto* | *Nerthe* |
| 1890 | *La Rèino Jano* | *La Reine Jeanne* — drama |

"Fine" literature fitting the 1890s southern-France window: the **1886–1921
Lemerre edition of Mistral's *Œuvres*** (Paris), and the completion of *Lou
Tresor dóu Felibrige* (through 1886). Mistral's autobiography *Moun espelido:
Memòri e raconte* came later (1906).

Sources: Nobel Prize bibliography/biography (nobelprize.org); Britannica
(Mistral; Félibrige); Musée Mistral Maillane; Internet Archive
(loutrsordufelibr01/02mist); Project Gutenberg #17293 (Downer, *Frédéric
Mistral*, 1901 — a public-domain study in English).

## 2. Original works from Italy, in and around 1491 — Leonardo da Vinci

In 1491 Leonardo was in **Milan**, in the service of **Ludovico Sforza** ("il
Moro"), Duke of Milan. Works and notebooks of that window:

- **Codex Forster III** (V&A, London) — compiled **c. 1490–1493** while Leonardo
  served Sforza; the most miscellaneous of his notebooks.
- **Codex Trivulzianus** (Biblioteca Trivulziana, Milan) — early Milan-period
  notebook; Leonardo's lists of learned words to improve his literary education.
- **The Sforza monument (Gran Cavallo)** — the colossal equestrian statue of
  Francesco Sforza; Leonardo worked on the clay model and casting plans across
  these years (never cast).
- Leonardo's famous **letter of self-introduction to Ludovico Sforza** predates
  this (c. 1482–1483), offering his engineering and artistic services.
- The **Codex Atlanticus** (Biblioteca Ambrosiana, Milan) is the largest single
  compilation of Leonardo's sheets and spans his career, including Milan years.

Sources: V&A (Codex Forster); Wikipedia (Codex Trivulzianus, Codex Atlanticus);
UNESCO Courier (Madrid Codices / the Sforza horse); Google Arts & Culture
(letter to Sforza); Project Gutenberg #5000 (*The Notebooks of Leonardo da
Vinci*, Richter trans. — public domain English edition).

### Experts / circle around Leonardo c. 1491

- **Ludovico Sforza** — his patron, Duke of Milan.
- **Luca Pacioli** — mathematician; later (from 1496) collaborated with Leonardo
  in Milan on *De divina proportione* (Leonardo drew its polyhedra). Just after
  the 1491 window, but the same Milanese circle.
- **Donato Bramante** — architect active at the Sforza court.

## 3. Printed Italian books of 1491 (incunabula)

Books printed before January 1501 are **incunabula**. Italy was a leading center
(Venice especially). Representative 1491 printing:

- **Dante, *Divina Commedia*** — many incunable editions appeared in Italy
  between the first (1472) and 1500 (about 15 editions), several in this window.
- ***Summa Angelica de casibus conscientiae*** (Angelo Carletti di Chivasso) —
  printed 1491; a widely held incunable.

Source: ASU Library (Dante incunable); Britannica & Wikipedia (incunabula);
University of Glasgow Special Collections (Summa Angelica, 1491).

## 4. The Church at the time — 1491 exactly

- **Pope Innocent VIII** (Giovanni Battista Cybo, 1432–1492) was head of the
  Catholic Church 1484–1492; he reigned in 1491. In **1491** the pope witnessed
  the imminent **fall of Granada** (Jan 1492), completing the Spanish
  Reconquista and earning Ferdinand & Isabella the title "Catholic Majesty."
- **Girolamo Savonarola** (1452–1498), the Dominican reformer, was elected
  **prior of San Marco in Florence in 1491** — the start of his rise; he was
  excommunicated and executed in 1498.
- Innocent VIII died 25 July 1492; **Alexander VI** (Borgia) was elected that
  August.

Sources: Catholic Encyclopedia / New Advent (Innocent VIII; Savonarola);
Wikipedia (Pope Innocent VIII; Savonarola); Christianity.com (Savonarola, prior
1491).
