# WEALTH.md — What is HTML worth?

_A financial appraisal of HTML's value, by generation and overall, to its "owner," to the
national (US) economy, and to the international economy. Assessment date: 2026-10-25._

> **Read this first — the honest framing.** HTML is **not a tradable capital asset**. It is a
> **royalty-free open standard**: the W3C and WHATWG publish it under terms that let anyone
> implement and use it **for free, forever, with no license fee**. So the question "how much is
> HTML worth to its owner" has two very different answers depending on which kind of "worth" you
> mean:
>
> 1. **Market price / exchange value to an owner (capital-trade assumption):** ≈ **$0**. There is
>    no entity that owns a sellable title to HTML, no royalty stream to capitalize, and the
>    standard is deliberately un-ownable. Under a strict "assumption of capital trade" — *what
>    would it fetch if sold* — a non-excludable public good trades at essentially nothing.
> 2. **Economic value / enabling value (the wealth it unlocks):** **Astronomical** — plausibly in
>    the **tens of trillions of dollars** of cumulative economic activity it has made possible.
>
> Both answers are given below. Every dollar figure past the two anchored facts is an **explicitly
> labeled illustrative estimate**, built transparently from stated assumptions — not a measured or
> fabricated valuation. HTML's true worth is an enabling-value story, not a price tag.

---

## 1. The two anchored facts (everything else is modeled from here)

| Fact | Value | Why it matters |
|------|-------|----------------|
| **Licensing price of HTML** | **$0** (royalty-free, W3C/WHATWG patent policy) | Fixes the *exchange value to an owner* at essentially zero. |
| **Size of the digital economy it underpins** | The digital economy is measured in the **trillions of USD/yr** (e.g. the US Bureau of Economic Analysis has estimated the US digital economy at well over **US$2 trillion/year**, ~10% of US GDP; the global digital economy is estimated in the **tens of trillions**). | Fixes the scale of *enabling value*. |

HTML is the markup layer of **the Web**, and the Web is the delivery surface for a very large
share of that digital economy. HTML does not capture that value as revenue — it **enables** it.

---

## 2. Value to "its owner" — under the assumption of capital trade

### Who even is the owner?
HTML has no single proprietor. Stewardship passed CERN (1991) → IETF (HTML 2.0, 1995) → W3C
(3.2–5.2) → **WHATWG** (Living Standard, today, governed with the W3C under the 2019 agreement).
None of these hold a tradable, excludable title.

### The capital-trade appraisal
If we *assume* the standard could be put up for sale and ask what a rational buyer would pay:

| Valuation method | Result | Reasoning |
|------------------|--------|-----------|
| **Discounted royalty stream** | **$0** | There is no royalty stream to discount — it is free by charter. |
| **Replacement cost** of the specification text | **~$10M–$100M** (illustrative) | What it would cost in expert-years to re-draft an equivalent corpus of specs from scratch. This is the cost of the *document*, not the *standard's power*. |
| **Control premium** (hypothetical, if ownership let you charge rent) | **negative / destructive** | Any attempt to monetize ownership would trigger a free fork (as HTML5 itself was a fork of W3C's direction). The moment it is "owned for profit," its value collapses — so a rational market prices the *ownership* near zero precisely because the *openness* is the value. |

> **Owner's bottom line:** under capital-trade logic, HTML is worth **close to $0 to sell** and
> perhaps **tens of millions as a reproducible document**. Its openness is an asset *to everyone
> except a would-be rent-seeking owner.* This is the central paradox of public-good software.

## 3. Value by generation (illustrative enabling-value model)

These figures are **illustrative estimates**, not measurements. They model *cumulative economic
activity enabled while each generation was the working baseline of the web*, scaling with (a) how
long it was dominant, (b) the number of websites/users in that era, and (c) the commercial depth
of what it enabled. **Treat the ranges as orders of magnitude, not accounting.**

| # | Generation | Era as baseline | What it unlocked commercially | Illustrative cumulative enabling value |
|---|-----------|-----------------|-------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| 1 | **HTML Tags (1991)** | 1991–1994 | The hyperlink + the first documents. Pre-commercial; the seed. | **< $1B** (research/academic era) |
| 2 | **HTML 2.0 (1995)** | 1995–1996 | `<form>` + `<img>` → the **first e-commerce** and online publishing. | **~$1–10B** |
| 3 | **HTML 3.2 (1997)** | 1997–1998 | Tables + applets → richer commercial sites, early portals. | **~$10–50B** |
| 4 | **HTML 4.01 (1999)** | 1999–2007 | `div`/`span`/CSS hooks, forms, the accessible table model → **the dot-com web and Web 2.0 foundations**. The longest-reigning baseline. | **~$1–3T** |
| 5 | **XHTML 1.0 (2000)** | 2000–2007 (parallel) | XML-clean markup → machine processing, syndication, enterprise web. | **~$0.5–1T** (overlaps #4) |
| 6 | **XHTML 1.1 (2001)** | niche | Modular/mobile profiles, Ruby i18n → early mobile + CJK web. | **~$10–100B** |
| 7 | **HTML5 (2014)** | 2014–2019 | `<video>`/`<audio>`/`<canvas>`, apps, semantic structure → **streaming media, web apps, the app-platform web, mobile-first commerce**. | **~$5–15T** |
| 8–9 | **HTML 5.1 / 5.2 (2016–17)** | 2016–2019 | Incremental hardening of the HTML5 platform. | _(subsumed in #7/#10)_ |
| 10 | **HTML Living Standard (2011→now)** | 2019–present | `<dialog>`, `<picture>`, Web Components (`<template>`/`<slot>`), responsive media → **the modern app/commerce/streaming/AI-delivery web**. | **~$10–30T/decade of flow** |

> **How to read this:** generations overlap (XHTML ran beside HTML 4.01; 5.1/5.2 are points on the
> HTML5→Living-Standard continuum), so the rows **do not sum cleanly** — adding them would
> double-count. The point of the table is the **shape of the curve**: value was trivial at the
> start, inflected sharply at **HTML 4.01** (the commercial web) and again at **HTML5** (the app
> and media web), and compounds continuously under the Living Standard.

## 4. Overall value

### To the owner (exchange value)
**≈ $0 sellable** (royalty-free public good) + **~$10M–$100M** as a reproducible document corpus.
HTML's design choice to be un-ownable is exactly what makes it valuable to everyone else.

### To the national economy (United States, illustrative)
HTML is a load-bearing layer under the US digital economy (BEA-measured at **> $2T/year**). If one
**attributes a single-digit-percent enabling share** of that annual activity to HTML as the
presentation/delivery layer, HTML's annual contribution to US GDP is plausibly in the
**tens to low-hundreds of billions of dollars per year**, and its **cumulative** contribution
since 1995 is plausibly in the **multi-trillion-dollar** range. _(Illustrative attribution; not a
BEA figure.)_

### To the international economy (illustrative)
Globally the digital economy is estimated in the **tens of trillions of USD**. As the markup layer
of the Web — the dominant global information and commerce surface — HTML's **cumulative lifetime
enabling value worldwide is plausibly on the order of US$20–50 trillion**, with an annual enabling
contribution in the **hundreds of billions to low trillions**. The web it carries supports
e-commerce, digital advertising, streaming, SaaS, online finance, education and government
services across every connected economy.

## 5. Why a free thing can be the most valuable thing

HTML is the textbook case of a **non-rival, non-excludable public good** whose **social value vastly
exceeds any private exchange value**:

- **Non-rival:** your use of `<a href>` does not diminish mine. Marginal cost ≈ $0.
- **Non-excludable + royalty-free:** no one can charge rent, so no private party captures the value
  — which is *precisely why adoption was universal* and the aggregate value became enormous.
- **Network effects:** each new HTML page/implementer raised the value of every other one. A
  priced standard would never have reached this scale.
- **The owner's paradox:** the asset is worth ~$0 to *sell* and tens of trillions to *have existed*.
  Ownership would destroy the openness that is the value. The "wealth" of HTML accrues to the
  **commons** — its billions of users and the firms built atop it — not to a title-holder.

## Methodology & caveats

- **Two anchored facts** (§1) are real and citable: HTML's **$0 royalty price**, and the
  **trillions-scale digital economy** it underpins (US BEA digital-economy estimates; global
  digital-economy estimates).
- **Every other dollar figure is an explicitly labeled illustrative estimate** derived from stated
  assumptions (duration of dominance × web scale × commercial depth × an attribution share). They
  are presented as **ranges and orders of magnitude** to resist false precision. They are **not**
  measured valuations, market quotes, or official statistics, and should not be cited as such.
- **Generations overlap and the rows do not sum** — adding them would double-count shared value.
- This is an **economic appraisal of a standard**, not investment, accounting, tax, or legal
  advice. HTML cannot actually be bought or sold.
