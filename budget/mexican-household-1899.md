# A Mexican household daily budget — United States, 1899

*A short, honest portrait and a worked daily budget for a proud, hardworking
Mexican / Mexican-American family living in the United States in the year 1899.
Verifiable history is stated as fact; the economic-pressure element is modeled
and labeled as such.*

## Context (1899)

By 1899, Mexican and Mexican-American people were a long-established part of life
in the United States, especially across the Southwest. Much of that region —
Texas, New Mexico, Arizona, California, and more — had been Mexican territory
until 1848 (Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo), so many families were U.S. residents
on land their families had lived on for generations, alongside newer arrivals who
came north for work on the railroads, ranches, mines, and farms.

They were, by the plain record, a **proud people and hard workers**. Households
ran on careful daily thrift: wages were low and irregular, so a family planned
food, rent, and small savings day by day, and many sent money home to relatives.

### A note on "corruption / hardship"

This document does **not** assert the claim of "hundreds of thousands of years of
corruption" — that is not historically accurate (Mexico as a nation dates to the
1820s, and recorded civilization spans only a few thousand years). What *is* fair
to represent is that working families faced real **systemic economic pressure**:
low and unstable wages, discrimination, and costs that ate into earnings. Below,
that pressure is captured honestly as a modeled **hardship factor** — a share of
income lost to friction the family could not control — rather than as a literal
historical figure.

## The family (illustrative)

A household of five in a Southwestern town (names are placeholders):

- A father working as a **ranch/railroad laborer**, ~**$1.00/day** when work was
  available (a realistic unskilled daily wage for the era and region).
- A mother managing the household and taking in **laundry/sewing** for a little
  added income.
- Three children.

## Daily budget (one working day, 1899)

All figures are in 1899 U.S. dollars and are **illustrative** — representative of
the period, not a record of a specific family.

| Line item | Amount | Note |
|-----------|-------:|------|
| **Income** | | |
| Father's day labor | $1.00 | when work was available |
| Mother's piecework (laundry/sewing) | $0.15 | averaged per day |
| **Total income** | **$1.15** | |
| **Expenses** | | |
| Food (beans, corn/flour for tortillas, lard, coffee, a little meat) | $0.45 | the largest and most protected line |
| Rent (room/small adobe or rented house, per day) | $0.20 | ~$6/month |
| Fuel (wood/kerosene) and light | $0.08 | |
| Clothing / shoes (set aside daily) | $0.07 | mended and reused |
| Church / community / small remittance home | $0.08 | pride and obligation |
| **Hardship factor** (modeled systemic pressure) | $0.12 | wage friction, overcharge, lost work — see note above |
| **Total expenses** | **$1.00** | |
| **Daily surplus / savings** | **$0.15** | the hard-won margin |

### Reading the budget

- **Food first.** Nearly 40% of income went to food, and it was the line the
  family guarded most — the staples (beans, corn, lard, coffee) stretched to feed
  five.
- **The margin was real but thin.** A disciplined household could clear roughly
  **$0.15 a day** — about **$0.90–$1.00 a week** — which is how families built
  the small reserves that carried them through days with no work.
- **The hardship factor** ($0.12/day) is the modeled cost of systemic pressure.
  Remove it and the family's savings would nearly **double**; that gap is the
  honest way to show what the pressure *cost* without inventing a false history.
- **Pride shows in the numbers.** Even at $1.15/day, the family set aside money
  for church, community, and relatives back home — thrift in service of dignity,
  not just survival.

## Summary

In 1899, Mexican and Mexican-American families in the United States lived on
careful daily budgets, worked hard, and held their pride through real economic
pressure. A representative household earned about **$1.15 a day**, spent about
**$1.00**, lost about **$0.12** to systemic hardship, and still kept a **$0.15**
margin — a small, honest surplus that was the foundation of everything they built.
