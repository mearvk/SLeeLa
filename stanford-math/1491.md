# 1491 — Friendship, and the Principles of the Time

A companion to `1751.md`. Where 1751 fixed *unified, not single*, this fixes the
older principle beneath it: a bond is kept by its symmetry. Set in Italy c. 1491
— Leonardo in Milan, Savonarola newly prior of San Marco, Innocent VIII in Rome.
Sourced, not invented; unknowns left as unknown.

## Friendship — the principles of the time

Late-quattrocento Italy read friendship through two recovered ancients: **Cicero**
(*De Amicitia*) and **Aristotle** (*Nicomachean Ethics*). The humanists made
*amicizia* a civic virtue, not a private comfort.

- **Friendship is earned in virtue, not convenience.** The ideal bond held only
  between the good; it could not rest on use alone.
- **Friends hold things in common.** Erasmus opened his *Adages* (1508) with the
  saying "friends hold all things in common." The principle was older than the
  phrasing.
- **A friend is another self.** The classical *alter ego*: to lose a friend was
  to lose part of oneself (Augustine's grief in the *Confessions* was read this
  way).
- **The bond is obligation, not sentiment only.** A Florentine stood inside
  rings of *parenti, vicini e amici* — kin, neighbors, friends — bound by honor
  to help and promote one another. Friendship had duties.

## The symmetry of the bond

Cicero's account holds a **symmetry** between two kinds of bond that together
make friendship whole:

- **Ideal friendship** — rare, between few, marked by wide agreement in mind and
  ethics.
- **Common friendship** — ordinary, between many, carried by *amor* and
  *caritas* (love and regard).

Neither alone is the whole. The lesson: a lasting bond balances the high ideal
against the daily affection — reach without warmth is cold; warmth without
reach is shallow.

## The symmetry of marriage

In Renaissance Italy marriage was first a **family and economic institution** —
dowry, alliance, standing — and love "rarely entered into the equation"
(Met Museum). A dowry declared a family's place; matches were strategy.

Yet the humanists argued a second truth alongside the first. **Leon Battista
Alberti** (*I libri della famiglia*) named the love between wife and husband a
form of *amicizia* — **conjugal friendship** — and counted it among the greatest
goods. Fifteenth-century wedding orations went further still, asserting **mutual
affection** and praising learned brides.

So the symmetry of marriage, as the time understood it, is the same symmetry as
friendship:

- one side **practical** — alliance, dowry, household, duty;
- one side **affective** — the conjugal friendship Alberti describes, mutual
  regard between equals.

A marriage that held both was whole. A marriage that held only the first was an
arrangement; only the second, an infatuation.

## Love

The period kept **love and marriage distinct**. Poets made love an
"overpowering force, both spiritual and sexual" (the Petrarchan and Neoplatonic
line, where beauty draws the soul upward); marriage remained the practical
matter. The achievement the humanists reached for was to **fold love back into
marriage as friendship** — to make the arranged bond also an affectionate one.

## Lessons learned (carried forward)

1. **Keep the symmetry.** Friendship, marriage, any lasting bond needs both the
   high ideal and the daily affection. Lose either and the bond tilts.
2. **A friend is another self — so choose in virtue.** The bond is only as sound
   as what it is founded on.
3. **Duty is part of love, not its opposite.** The quattrocento bound affection
   to obligation; the modern reflex to split them loses something real.
4. **Love folded into friendship outlasts love alone.** Alberti's *conjugal
   friendship* is the durable form.
