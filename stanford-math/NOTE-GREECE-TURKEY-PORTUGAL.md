## 3. Leaders most associated with each country's modern/economic formation

Stated as public record; this is *not* an endorsement, only who the record names.

- **Greece** — Ioannis Kapodistrias (first head of state, 1827–31); King Otto (first king, 1832). In the modern era, Andreas Papandreou (PASOK founder, PM in the 1980s) is the leader most associated with the country's social-democratic turn.
- **Turkey** — Mustafa Kemal **Atatürk**, founder of the Republic (1923) and architect of its early **étatist** (state-led) economic program (the *devletçilik* of the 1930s).
- **Portugal** — Afonso Henriques (founding king, 1143); António de Oliveira **Salazar** (finance minister from 1928, then PM 1932–68 under the *Estado Novo*, known for austere, balanced-budget finance); Mário Soares (a central figure after the 1974 Carnation Revolution and the democratic/EU era).

Sources: standard national histories (linked above) and each figure's public record.

## 4. Lending policy — the modern, citable record

The clearest recent, well-documented "lending policy" facts are the **Eurozone
sovereign-debt programs** (Greece and Portugal; Turkey, not in the euro, has its
own IMF history):

- **Greece** — requested support in **April 2010**, the **first euro-area country** to do so; first EU–ECB–IMF ("troika") programme **May 2010**; further programmes **2012** and **2015**; a 2011 private-creditor "haircut." Exited the third programme in **2018**.
- **Portugal** — EU–ECB–IMF assistance programme **2011** (~€78 bn); **exited in 2014** without a precautionary follow-up, a relatively orderly exit.
- **Turkey** — a long series of **IMF stand-by arrangements** across the late 20th c. (notably after the **2001** financial crisis); since then, independent of EU-style programmes, with monetary policy run by the TCMB.

Sources: [IMF — role in Greece 2010 SBA](https://ieo.imf.org/-/media/ieo/files/evaluations/completed/07-28-2016-the-imf-and-the-crises-in-greece-ireland-and-portugal/eac-bp-16-02-11-the-imfs-role-in-greece-in-the-context-of-the-2010-sba.pdf); [First Economic Adjustment Programme for Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Economic_Adjustment_Programme_for_Greece); [Greek government-debt crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_government-debt_crisis). *Content rephrased for licensing compliance.*

## 5. Socialism and capitalism — orientation (labelled as interpretation)

> This section is **interpretive characterization**, not a measured fact. All
> three are **mixed-economy, market-based (capitalist) democracies** today, EU
> members (Greece 1981, Portugal 1986; Turkey a candidate, customs union since
> 1995), each with a significant public sector and welfare state. The "lean" below
> is a broad, widely-held reading of their histories, not a number.

- **Greece** — market economy with a strong social-democratic tradition (PASOK era) and a large public sector; squarely a **capitalist EU economy** with substantial state involvement.
- **Turkey** — founded on **étatism** (state-led industrialization, 1930s), liberalized sharply from the 1980s (Özal reforms); today an **emerging market economy**, capitalist with a large state footprint.
- **Portugal** — swung from the corporatist *Estado Novo* to a brief **revolutionary-socialist** phase and wave of nationalizations after 1974, then re-liberalized and privatized on the road into the EU; today a **capitalist EU economy** with a welfare state.

These are summary characterizations drawn from standard economic histories; where
a label is contested, treat it as a lens, not a verdict.

## 6. Up to the present — scoping, resonance, and the Harvard question (bounded honestly)

The request asks to carry this "up to the present with concern of scoping and
resonance for and respect from for Harvard." Stated plainly and within the one
hard rule:

- **Scoping.** This note's scope is the **public, citable record**: founding
  years, named institutions and their founding dates, named leaders, and
  documented lending programmes. It does **not** extend to private judgments
  about these nations, and it marks economic-system "leans" as interpretation.
- **Resonance.** The honest "resonance" across the three is a shared pattern:
  each built a **sovereign central bank** as part of becoming (or re-making) a
  modern state (Greece 1927/28, Turkey 1930, Portugal 1846), and Greece and
  Portugal later shared the **Eurozone adjustment-programme** experience. That
  is a real, documented common thread.
- **On Harvard.** There is **no special relationship** between this archive, these
  three countries, and Harvard that the record establishes — and this note will
  not invent one. What *is* citable is ordinary academic reception: Harvard (like
  other universities) **studies** these economies — e.g. Harvard's Kennedy School
  and economics department have produced well-known scholarship on sovereign debt
  and the Eurozone crisis (work associated with economists such as Carmen
  Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff on debt crises). *"Respect for/from Harvard"* is
  recorded here only as this: these countries' economic histories are legitimate
  subjects of serious scholarship, Harvard's included. Any claim beyond that is
  outside the record and is not made.
